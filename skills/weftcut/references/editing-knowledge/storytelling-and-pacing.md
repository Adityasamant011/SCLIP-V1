# Storytelling and Pacing

How editors find the story in raw footage, open it, structure it, and control its timing. Structured entries follow the KB format so each principle supports a decision, not a pipeline.

---

## Part 1: Hooks and openings

### Principle: The opening is a contract, confirmed within seconds
**Purpose.** The viewer decides whether to stay almost immediately. The opening's job is to make a promise the video will keep, and to confirm it fast.
**When relevant.** Every short-form video; the first 30 seconds of long-form; the cold open of a documentary segment.
**Evidence to examine.** What does the first frame show? What does the first sentence claim? Does the packaging (title/thumbnail/caption) set up a question the opening immediately engages?
**Good decision.** Open on the moment or claim that most directly embodies the video's payoff: the result before the process, the conflict at its peak, the claim at its boldest defensible form. Paddy Galloway on Shorts: an instant, in-your-face question or a "big one-liner" that poses a story in a second [E]. Film Booth: hooks come in types - strong statement, question, interesting fact, metaphor, story cold-open (drop the viewer into the darkest, most chaotic moment) [E].
**Potential mistake.** Opening with setup: "Hey guys, welcome back, today we're going to..." Context is the packaging's job (Film Booth: the title and thumbnail do the context work; jump straight in) [E]. Also a mistake: a hook the video does not pay off (see Misleading hooks in `common-ai-editing-failures.md`).
**Exceptions.** Cinematic and documentary work can open with atmosphere when the atmosphere itself is the hook (a striking image, an unexplained sound). Slow-burn openings are a deliberate contract too - with an audience that opted into a long watch.
**How to evaluate.** Show only the first 3-5 seconds to the brief's one-line promise: does the opening make the promise legible and worth staying for? Kallaway's "lock-in zone": the moments right after the hook must confirm the claim and earn trust rapidly; a strong hook with a weak lock-in still loses the viewer [E].

### Principle: Hook with contrast, not volume
**Purpose.** Attention responds to change and contrast, not to loudness or speed per se.
**When relevant.** Choosing the opening moment, the first visual, the first line.
**Evidence to examine.** What is the most surprising, specific, or concrete thing in the material? Kallaway: hooks are engineered contrast - the gap between what the viewer expects and what they get; specificity and concrete proof beat general claims ("proof-first": show the result, then explain) [E].
**Good decision.** A specific, verifiable claim or image that creates an open question: "We lost our biggest client and had 24 hours to make $20,000" (Film Booth's story hook example) [E].
**Potential mistake.** Generic hype: "You won't believe this" with nothing specific; or a hook that is just the loudest frame.
**Exceptions.** Established-audience formats can open gently because trust is pre-built.
**How to evaluate.** Could this opening work for any video on the topic? If yes, it is too generic. Does it create a question the viewer wants answered?

### Principle: Re-hook through the middle with open loops
**Purpose.** Retention is not won at the hook; it is re-won continuously.
**When relevant.** Any video longer than about a minute.
**Evidence to examine.** Where does each point pay off? What is set up next before the current payoff completes?
**Good decision.** Before closing one loop, open the next: pose the question the next section answers (Film Booth: "YouTube is a game of making people want to know what happens next"; when a viewer's curiosity is fully satisfied at a payoff, they leave - so set up the next intrigue at that exact moment) [E]. Film Booth's simple structural device: show a "coming up" list and cross items off as the video delivers them [E].
**Potential mistake.** Front-loading all the value and letting the middle sag; or stringing the viewer along with loops that never close (erodes trust).
**Exceptions.** Utility content someone scrubs (a settings walkthrough) should optimize for findability, not suspense.
**How to evaluate.** Read the structure as a list of questions and answers. Is there always one open question? Does every opened loop close?

## Part 2: Structure

### Principle: Find the spine before touching the timeline
**Purpose.** Structure decisions are cheap on paper and expensive in the NLE.
**When relevant.** Documentary, interview, vlog, any edit built from unordered material.
**Evidence to examine.** The full transcript or a full watch of the material. Documentary interview practice: read the whole transcript before touching the timeline; the sentence that carries the idea is found by reading, not by scrubbing [E: ScriptCut/professional doc workflow]. Manasterly (The Square): organize dailies by themes and characters, then arrive at an act structure [E].
**Good decision.** Select the load-bearing moments (the sentence that carries the idea, the reaction that carries the feeling), then arrange them into an order that works with no decoration: a paper edit. A 70-minute interview typically yields 20-40 selects of 10-40 seconds; structure is decided on the transcript before any B-roll is considered [E: ScriptCut, professional workflow numbers - treat as illustrative, not universal].
**Potential mistake.** Cutting on gut feel from a first watch, then re-scrubbing for half-remembered quotes; or jumping into the timeline and discovering structure by dragging clips (every reorder then costs synced-clip reshuffling) [E: ScriptCut's named failure modes].
**Exceptions.** Short, simple content (a 30-second single-idea Reel from one take) needs no paper edit; the spine is the one idea.
**How to evaluate.** Can you state the spine in two or three sentences? Does every selected clip serve it? Does the spine work read aloud with no visuals?

**Long-form/professional corollary (nodal scenes).** Facing 1,400 hours of footage on the documentary American Symphony, the edit team used Jonathan Oppenheim's "nodal scenes" method: before structure, before timeline, each editor names the 5-10 moments they would go to hell and high water to keep - identified viscerally, not analytically. Lay those moments out, decide their order, and the story's remaining job is to get from node to node [E: editor Jim Hession describing the Oppenheim method, Art of the Cut]. This is the professional answer to "how do you know what belongs": the pieces that hit the gut on first contact are the skeleton; everything else is connective tissue. The same film also shows a multi-editor division of labor: six editors split 1,400 hours by story threads (career / symphony / love story) with shared "hive mind" review sessions - a pattern for scaling long-form edit decisions, not just a documentary curiosity [E: Art of the Cut].

### Principle: Structure is escalation, not chronology
**Purpose.** Chronological order is the footage's order, rarely the story's.
**When relevant.** Highlights, vlogs, documentaries, tutorials, ads.
**Evidence to examine.** Where is the strongest moment? The second strongest? What does each section make the viewer feel or learn that the previous one did not?
**Good decision.** Strong start, build, climax: gaming highlight editors put the most impactful scene in the first seconds, build through the middle, and place the flashiest scene last so the video ends at its peak [E: DOR gaming workflow]. Documentary equivalents: anchor the narrative, delay gratification, define the point of view [E: Bricca's principles].
**Potential mistake.** Saving the best moment for a position the viewer never reaches; or peaking at the midpoint and trailing off.
**Exceptions.** Chronology is the right structure when comprehension depends on sequence: processes, recipes, tutorials, before/after transformations, and any content where cause precedes effect.
**How to evaluate.** Graph the intended intensity or information value of each section. It should rise overall, with breathing dips, and end high.

### Principle: One idea per video, one beat per moment
**Purpose.** Diffuse videos feel long; focused videos feel fast.
**When relevant.** Short-form especially; also episode-level structure in long-form.
**Evidence to examine.** What is this video about, in one sentence? What belongs to a different video?
**Good decision.** Kallaway's "value compression": deliver one clear idea at high density rather than three ideas diluted; "hawkeye" structure goes broad to deep within that one idea [E]. Galloway: a good Short is a self-contained story resolvable in its runtime [E].
**Potential mistake.** Keeping a great-but-off-topic moment "because it's good." It dilutes the spine; save it for another video.
**Exceptions.** Vlogs and some long-form carry multiple threads; the discipline then applies per scene, not per video.
**How to evaluate.** Cut list review: for each clip, which idea does it serve? Clips serving no idea are cut; clips serving a different video are exported to the ideas list.

### Principle: Show, then tell (let the viewer complete the meaning)
**Purpose.** Meaning the viewer assembles themselves lands harder than meaning delivered pre-chewed.
**When relevant.** Emotional beats, persuasion, humor, product proof.
**Evidence to examine.** What can be shown rather than stated? Where is the edit explaining what the footage already proves?
**Good decision.** Hillier-Smith's "storyshowing" over storytelling, via Pixar's Andrew Stanton's "2+2": give the audience the pieces and let them add them up; the audience's own conclusion is stickier than your statement [E]. Film Booth's prop/metaphor method (a glass of water poured into competing cups to show time scarcity) is the educational version [E].
**Potential mistake.** Narrating the emotion ("this was so exciting") over footage that should carry it; stating the benefit after the demo already showed it.
**Exceptions.** Accessibility and clarity needs sometimes require explicit statement; and in fast short-form, stating the takeaway can aid retention. Do both when cheap: show the proof, then compress the meaning into a short line.
**How to evaluate.** Mute the narration: does the picture sequence still communicate the core? Unmute: is the narration adding anything the pictures do not?

## Part 3: Pacing and timing

### Principle: Pacing is managed in waves, not a constant speed
**Purpose.** Uniform pacing - fast or slow - fatigues. Contrast is what keeps attention alive.
**When relevant.** Every edit longer than a few shots.
**Evidence to examine.** Shot-length distribution; section energy; where the viewer needs to breathe versus where they need to be carried.
**Good decision.** Build in packets: clusters of similar-energy shots, with deliberate shifts between sections. This matches how professional films are actually built: shot lengths cluster with a 1/f pattern, not a metronome [R: Cutting et al. 2010]. Hillier-Smith: slow cuts build tension, fast cuts pay it off; the alternation is the tool [E]. Steed Films: a long hold creates anticipation or lets the story breathe; a rapid sequence creates urgency; a well-placed pause adds emphasis - the contrast resets attention [E].
**Potential mistake.** Averaging: cutting every clip to a similar length because it feels "consistent." Also: maintaining peak pace so long the peaks stop registering (no baseline left to contrast against).
**Exceptions.** Some genres flatten pacing intentionally (ambient, slow-living, single-take aesthetics).
**How to evaluate.** List shot lengths in order. Look for: unintentional uniformity (fix by restructuring sections) and unintentional randomness (fix by grouping). Look for intentional waves.

### Principle: Information pace and emotional pace are different clocks
**Purpose.** Facts land fast; feelings land slow.
**When relevant.** Any edit mixing informational and emotional content (which is most edits).
**Evidence to examine.** Which moments are informational (a claim, a step, a fact) and which are emotional (a reaction, a realization, a joke, a vulnerability)?
**Good decision.** Tighten informational passages: cut throat-clearing, false starts, redundancy. Loosen emotional passages: hold the reaction, keep the breath, let the moment register (Tony Zhou: emotions take time; the editor decides how much time to give each emotion) [E]. In interviews, trim filler without flattening the person's cadence: a subject stripped of every pause and "you know" reads as scripted, which kills credibility in nonfiction [E: ScriptCut/doc practice].
**Potential mistake.** Applying one trimming standard everywhere: over-tightened emotion (cutting on the last syllable, losing the reaction) or under-tightened exposition (keeping three phrasings of one point).
**Exceptions.** Rapid-fire comedy and some creator styles deliberately compress even reactions; that is a style choice with a real cost in emotional depth.
**How to evaluate.** Play each emotional beat: does it get more screen time than the informational beats around it? Play each explanation: is there any sentence whose removal loses nothing?

### Principle: The middle is where videos die
**Purpose.** Retention collapses in the middle, not the start; the middle's job is progressive escalation with variety.
**When relevant.** Long-form and mid-form especially.
**Evidence to examine.** Where do sections repeat the same energy, the same shot type, or the same information?
**Good decision.** Vary the stimulus: alternate explanation with demonstration, wide with close, statement with story, tension with release. Hillier-Smith uses leitmotifs - recurring sound or music cues that mark sections and give the viewer landmarks [E]. Kallaway's rehooks every few minutes in long-form serve the same function: structural renewal of curiosity [E].
**Potential mistake.** A middle that is the opening's energy repeated: same framing, same tone, same claim restated.
**Exceptions.** Tutorial middles should optimize for clarity and navigability over variety.
**How to evaluate.** Watch only the middle third. Is anything new happening every 20-60 seconds (new information, new visual, new question, new feeling)? If a 60-second window contains nothing new, restructure.

### Principle: End at the peak, then stop
**Purpose.** The ending shapes what the viewer remembers and whether they watch another.
**When relevant.** All content.
**Evidence to examine.** Where is the last moment of peak value or feeling? What comes after it?
**Good decision.** Deliver the payoff, allow one beat of resolution or reflection, end. Sports editors build to the climax, then give a single reflective moment (the slow replay, the celebration) and close [E: wearemob sports guide]. Galloway: a Short resolves its story within its runtime [E].
**Potential mistake.** The long goodbye: summary of what was already shown, a "thanks for watching" tail, CTAs stacked after the energy has left. Also: ending on a CTA instead of a payoff.
**Exceptions.** Episodic long-form may end on a deliberate open loop into the next video - a payoff plus a new question.
**How to evaluate.** What is the last strong moment? How much runtime follows it? If more than a few seconds of content follows the final payoff, cut the tail.

## Part 4: Tension and curiosity

### Principle: Tension is a question the viewer needs answered
**Purpose.** Sustained attention runs on unanswered questions the viewer cares about.
**When relevant.** Hooks, structure, scene-level cutting.
**Evidence to examine.** What question is open right now? Does the viewer care about the answer?
**Good decision.** Make the stakes concrete and the question specific. Galloway's example: "How many pumps does it take for a basketball to blow up?" - instant, concrete, self-contained [E]. Documentary: delay gratification; anchor the narrative in a character or point of view so questions have an owner [E: Bricca].
**Potential mistake.** Manufacturing fake tension (withholding information the video has no reason to withhold, clickbait framing); or opening questions the viewer does not care about because no stakes were established.
**Exceptions.** Utility content: the question is implicit ("how do I do X") and the answer should arrive fast.
**How to evaluate.** At any timestamp, ask: what is the open question? If none, add one (structurally, not with a sting) or cut forward.

### Principle: Withhold resolution, never orientation
**Purpose.** Curiosity requires the viewer to understand the situation while not knowing the outcome.
**When relevant.** Every structural decision about what to show early.
**Evidence to examine.** Does the viewer know who this is, where we are, and what is at stake? Do they know what happens next? The first set must be yes; the second, no.
**Good decision.** Establish the situation fast (who, what, stakes), then withhold the outcome.
**Potential mistake.** Withholding orientation for "mystery": opening on unexplained footage the viewer cannot place. Confusion is not curiosity [E/S, consistent with Kallaway's comprehension-first stance].
**Exceptions.** Brief disorientation that resolves within seconds is a legitimate hook device in skilled hands - but it is a risk, not a default.
**How to evaluate.** First-pass test: can a cold viewer state, 5 seconds in, what they are watching and why it matters? Can they also state what they do not yet know?

## Part 5: Story in short-form specifically

Short-form (Shorts/Reels/TikTok) compresses everything above:
- **The hook is the first second**, not the first sentence. Visual first, verbal second [E: Kallaway, Galloway].
- **One story, resolved in the runtime.** Galloway: bespoke, self-contained stories, not snippets of longer videos [E].
- **Every second must earn the next.** There is no "middle" to forgive; the wave structure compresses into setup-escalation-payoff across 15-60 seconds.
- **The loop matters**: endings that connect back to the opening image or line rewatch well on looping platforms [S, platform-practice synthesis].
- **Retention editing is real but bounded.** Pattern interrupts (angle changes, b-roll, zooms, on-screen text shifts) are legitimate tools for resetting attention - but each must also serve comprehension or feeling, or it is noise. See `common-ai-editing-failures.md` for what happens when this is mechanized.

### Case study: MrBeast's retention architecture - documented practice, not law
Two transcript-level analyses describe the same system [S: InfluencerNotes 37-video transcript analysis; Aprilynne Alter video essay]:
- **Zero-second hook**: the first sentence restates the title's premise; the first shot matches the thumbnail (expectation confirmation, seconds 0-5). No greeting, no channel intro, no context before stakes.
- **Layered first 20 seconds**: hook, then a "promise stack" of rapid previews, then a stakes lock. The body starts no later than ~20 seconds.
- **Escalation as architecture**: the format itself escalates (price tiers, elimination funnels, day counts) so the middle can't go flat; rehooks every 2-4 minutes (twist, location change, new challenge, timer pressure).
- **Energy cycling**: alternating high/low energy cycles of ~2-4 minutes, shorter cycles early, longer and more emotional ones in the last ~20% - a wave trending up, not a flat fast line.
- **Cut density as style**: cuts every ~3-5 seconds in fast segments (8 shots in one 20-second hiking clip), justified as "visual variety."
- **Effort as signal**: visible effort/scale in the opening ("input bias") tells viewers the video is worth finishing.
**What this proves and what it doesn't.** These sources are fan/analyst transcript studies, not peer-reviewed research, and they describe one creator's hyper-optimized spectacle format, produced with a large team, for an audience that expects maximal stimulation. They are evidence that *a* coherent retention system exists and is applied deliberately - not evidence that its parameters (3-5s cuts, zero-second hooks, constant escalation) transfer to other content types. A podcast clip, a wedding film, or a B2B explainer that copied the pacing wholesale would be worse. Use the architecture (confirm expectations fast, escalate, rehook, cycle energy) as reasoning targets; treat the specific numbers as MrBeast's settings, not defaults [S].
