# Editorial Taste: Judging Choices and Knowing When Not to Edit

Taste is the ability to tell, in a specific situation, which of several competent options is right - and when the right move is to do nothing. It cannot be reduced to a checklist, but it can be trained, described, and reasoned about. This document is Hermes' guide to that reasoning.

---

## Part 1: What taste is (and is not)

**What the evidence says.**
- Editors describe their own craft as trained instinct. Tony Zhou: "Like a lot of editors, I cut based on instinct." His method for building that instinct: "If you watch anything over and over again, you eventually feel the moment the shot wants you to cut" [E: Every Frame a Painting, "How Does an Editor Think and Feel?"]. Walter Murch, in the same film: "Editing is 70% about rhythm."
- Taste is comparative, not absolute. It operates by auditioning alternatives against each other and against the brief, not by matching a template.
- Taste is learnable through exposure and feedback: editors build it by watching dailies, noting their own first responses, and testing cuts against audiences [E: Frame.io editorial series].

**What taste is not.**
- Not a style. Minimalist and maximalist editors both have taste; taste is the quality of decisions within a style. Sclip's style-learning system owns creator-specific preferences (this creator's caption look, their pacing signature). This document owns the layer below: decisions that are better or worse regardless of style.
- Not rule-following. An edit can satisfy every mechanical rule and still feel dead; another can break rules and sing (Murch's hierarchy exists precisely because priorities trade off) [E].

## Part 2: Recognizing specific taste failures

Each entry: what it looks like, why it happens, and the test.

### The unnecessary effect
**Looks like.** A zoom, transition, sting, or animation that would not be missed if removed.
**Why it happens.** Defaulting to "add energy" instead of solving a specific problem; template libraries; fear of stillness.
**Test.** Remove it mentally (or literally) and play the moment. If nothing is lost - no clarity, no feeling, no rhythm - the element was decoration. Decoration at best wastes attention; at worst it signals amateur or automated origin [E/S; consistent with Mayer coherence, R].

### The awkward cut
**Looks like.** A cut that "bumps": the viewer feels the edit instead of the story.
**Why it happens.** Cutting against movement (in dead stillness when motion was available), mid-breath or mid-phoneme in speech, between near-identical framings, or at a moment with no information or emotional justification.
**Test.** Play the cut at full speed three times without scrubbing. A good cut disappears or lands as rhythm; an awkward cut snags every time. Then check the usual suspects: motion phase, breath, framing distance, sound [E/S, grounded in R: Smith et al. 2012 on motion-masked cuts].

### The overly long moment
**Looks like.** A shot, pause, or section that continues after it has delivered.
**Why it happens.** Attachment to material; confusing "cinematic" with "slow"; not watching the cut as a first-time viewer.
**Test.** At each moment ask: has the viewer received what this is giving? A shot read in two seconds that holds for six is a drain - unless the hold itself is the point (tension, awkwardness, atmosphere). The distinction: a purposeful hold builds pressure; an accidental one leaks it [E/S; Zhou's "emotions take time" is the counterweight - do not confuse fat with feeling].

### The unnatural transition
**Looks like.** A dissolve, wipe, or fancy move between two shots that had no relationship requiring it.
**Why it happens.** Reaching for a transition to fix a structural problem (weak juxtaposition, missing scene logic). Transitions cannot fix structure.
**Test.** Replace the transition with a hard cut. If the cut now feels wrong, the problem is the shot pairing or order, not the missing transition. If it feels fine, the transition was decoration (E: consistent with professional hard-cut-default practice).

### The generic-looking edit
**Looks like.** Competent, forgettable, indistinguishable from thousands of other videos: stock B-roll, standard template zooms, default captions, library music, uniform pacing.
**Why it happens.** Optimizing each element to the average of what exists. An unguided process lands on the highest-probability option, and the highest-probability option is by definition the most average one [E: the generative-AI "drift toward the mean" critique applies directly to template editing].
**Test.** The swap test: could this exact edit serve a different creator or a different topic with a find-and-replace? If yes, it has no point of view. The fix is not more effects; it is more specific decisions - footage-specific cutaways, the creator's actual language, deliberate pacing choices, music chosen against the scene.

### The caption that distracts
**Looks like.** Text that pulls the eye off the subject at the wrong moment, covers the face or the product, or demands reading while something must be watched.
**Why it happens.** Caption systems optimized for word-accuracy styling over comprehension; placement defaults that ignore the picture.
**Test.** Watch the sequence as a viewer who cannot hear: can they get everything from picture + captions without whiplash? Then as a viewer who cannot read the captions (fast glance only): did the captions steal the picture's key moment? BBC's positioning and two-line discipline exists precisely to manage this [R/standard: BBC].

### B-roll that adds nothing
**Looks like.** Generic office typing, coffee pours, skyline shots, stock handshakes under specific narration.
**Why it happens.** Filling a perceived quota ("time for B-roll") instead of answering the narration.
**Test.** The sentence-pairing test: play the narration line and the cutaway together. Does the picture make the sentence more concrete, more credible, or more felt? If the pairing is arbitrary, cut back to the speaker or find the shot that actually answers the line [E: editorcuts].

### Music that undermines the emotion
**Looks like.** A track whose emotional content contradicts or flattens the scene: triumphant music under bad news, generic uplift under everything.
**Why it happens.** Choosing music by category instead of by audition against the scene.
**Test.** Research says the audience reads the scene partly through the music [R: film-music studies]. So: name the feeling of the scene in one word; name the feeling of the track in one word. If the words differ, the music is rewriting the scene - acceptable only as a deliberate, ironic choice. Audition at least two alternatives before settling [E: Steed].

## Part 3: Comparing treatments

The practical engine of taste is structured comparison. When multiple options are plausible:

1. **Generate real alternatives, not variants.** A different music track, a different opening moment, a different order - not three lengths of the same trim.
2. **Test against the brief's one-line intent.** Each alternative either serves the stated purpose better or worse. "Which is more engaging?" is unanswerable; "which makes the promise clearer in the first 5 seconds?" is answerable.
3. **Test one variable at a time where possible.** Same cut, two music tracks. Same music, two openings. This is how editors actually audition (Steed: play the rough cut with different songs and notice how each activates a different flavor of feeling) [E].
4. **Watch at full speed, in context.** Slow review and looped micro-trimming produce cuts that are technically smooth and felt-wrong; rhythm judgments are only valid at playback speed [E: ToolsForFilm's drift note; professional practice].
5. **Prefer the option that is doing more with less.** Given two options that achieve the same effect, the one with fewer elements wins (coherence principle, plus professional "less is more" consensus) [R + E].
6. **When alternatives are genuinely equal, the decision is style, not quality - defer to the creator's learned style profile.** Do not manufacture a quality argument to break a style tie.

## Part 4: When NOT to edit

The strongest form of restraint. Do not intervene when:

- **The raw moment is already doing the work.** A genuine reaction, an unscripted laugh, a perfectly framed action. Cutting it up, zooming it, or scoring over it subtracts. Hillier-Smith's Civil War example: the power was in letting the audience settle into the film's own language - restraint as the active choice [E].
- **The pause is the point.** The silence before an answer, the beat after a confession. Removing it for pace destroys the content.
- **The "imperfection" is the credibility.** A subject's hesitations, a handheld wobble in documentary, a raw room tone: these signal authenticity. Cleaning them out can make true footage feel staged [E: ScriptCut's cadence warning; doc practice].
- **You cannot name the problem.** "It feels like it needs something" is not a diagnosis. If no specific defect or opportunity can be stated, the correct action is to leave it and move on.
- **The footage is simple and the idea is clear.** Over-editing simple footage is a documented AI failure mode; a clean single-take delivery of a strong idea needs almost nothing (see `common-ai-editing-failures.md`).

## Part 5: General quality vs. creator style (the boundary)

Sclip learns each creator's style separately. This knowledge base must not smuggle style in as "quality."

| Layer | Examples | Who owns it |
|---|---|---|
| General quality | Intelligible audio; captions readable; cuts motivated; story coherent; music fits scene emotion; no objective defects | This KB |
| Genre conventions | Beat-synced sports reels; jump-cut vlogs; dense meme captions | `content-types.md` (as documented practice, not law) |
| Creator style | This creator's exact caption look, their pacing signature, their music taste, their humor density | Sclip's style-learning system |

Rule of thumb: if two skilled editors could reasonably disagree, it is style. If virtually all skilled editors would call it a defect (clipped dialogue, unreadable captions, a hook the video doesn't pay off), it is quality. When uncertain, classify as style and defer [S].
