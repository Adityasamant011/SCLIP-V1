# Content Types: Format-Specific Editorial Guidance

Principles in `editing-foundations.md` are universal; their weighting and defaults change by format. This document sets the defaults per format: what the audience came for, what good looks like, how selection and pacing differ, and which mistakes are common. Do not port one format's grammar into another (the classic error: editing a documentary interview like a hype reel).

**Universal across all types (do not re-litigate per type):** intelligible dialogue; captions readable and synced; cuts motivated; music serving scene emotion; story spine before decoration; restraint as default. What follows is only what changes.

---

## 1. Talking-head / educational

### Case study: what engagement data says about educational video [R]
Analysis of 6.9 million edX viewing sessions (Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014) found: engagement falls steeply with length (invest in breaking content into sub-6-minute chunks); talking-head shots interleaved with slides engaged more than slides alone; informal, personal-feeling filming beat high-production studio recording; and instructors who spoke fairly fast with high enthusiasm engaged more than slower speakers. Lectures and tutorial-style content behaved differently: tutorials benefited from explicit "chunking" support, lectures from watching support. Caveat: MOOC engagement is not social-media retention, and these are correlations from one platform - treat as evidence for educational formats, not as universal rules.

**What the audience came for.** To learn or understand something specific, fast, from someone they trust.

**Selection.** Find the strongest single idea; the video is that idea. Kallaway: value compression - one idea at high density beats three diluted [E]. Select the clearest phrasing of each point, not the most passionate.

**Structure.** Hook that makes the payoff legible (strong statement, question, or fact - Film Booth's hook types [E]); then hawkeye: broad orientation, then depth [E: Kallaway]. Skip throat-clearing intros; the packaging did the context (Film Booth) [E]. Middle: one idea per beat, open the next loop before closing the current one. End on the payoff, not the recap.

**Pacing.** Informational clock: tight. Remove false starts, repeated phrasings, filler that adds nothing - but keep the speaker's personality tics that add warmth. Emphasis moments (the key sentence) get a beat of air after them.

**Visuals.** Say it and show it at the same time: a few words of text or a simple graphic synced with the claim (Film Booth's simplest effective move; supported by signaling and temporal contiguity research, ES 0.70 / 1.31) [E + R]. Metaphors and props compress complexity (Film Booth's water-glass example) [E]. B-roll as evidence for claims, not decoration. Jump cuts at thought boundaries are native grammar here.

**Audio.** Voice first, always. Music low and supportive or absent; silence is fine.

**Common mistakes.** Making the viewer feel dumb (over-depth, jargon before definition - Film Booth's #1 educational error) [E]; re-explaining the title in the first 20 seconds; three phrasings of one point kept "for clarity"; captions duplicating full narration (redundancy cost) [R: Mayer].

**Do not port in.** Sports-reel pacing and beat-synced cutting; heavy sound design.

## 2. Podcasts / interviews (conversational)

### Case study: Diary of a CEO trailers and intros
- Anthony Smith (former DOAC Director of Trailers, ~450 trailers) describes the job as documentary thinking, not highlight-reel assembly: early drafts failed because they were "a clip and another clip and another clip"; the fix was building *bridges* between moments so the trailer reads as one condensed story. Every craft decision was derived from the intended emotion (the show as "a place for completely open, uncomfortable conversations") - even the famous metallic riser existed to make viewers feel discomfort, and was retired once imitators used it as decoration [E: Anthony Smith interview]. His core warning: "It's not the style that needs to be done. It's the mindsets behind it" - don't copy another show's sound design or structure; ask what your own show makes people feel first.
- An independent reverse-engineering of the DOAC intro format identifies six repeatable parts: cold-open sound-bite ("I lost two hundred million in a single day"), one-breath narrator credibility line (under 15 words), origin tease, three rapid-fire theme questions with under-10s answer teases, a mid-reveal cliff-hanger cut into theme music, all inside roughly 90 seconds [S: Medium analyst breakdown - one person's analysis, not DOAC documentation].
**What this proves / doesn't prove:** Smith's account is a first-person practitioner source for *why* the edits were made; the six-part formula is a third-party pattern extraction. Together they support "story-shaped clip with a cold-open hook" for podcast trailers; they do not establish it as optimal for all podcasts.

**What the audience came for.** Insight, personality, the chemistry between people. In clips: the single best exchange.

**Selection.** Read before cutting: the load-bearing sentence is found by reading the transcript, not scrubbing [E: ScriptCut]. Select exchanges, not monologues, when the interplay is the value; select the cleanest complete thought when the insight is. A 70-minute interview yields roughly 20-40 selects of 10-40 seconds on first pass (professional doc workflow figure - illustrative, not a rule) [E: ScriptCut].

**Structure.** Paper-edit the spine from selects before touching the NLE; arrange for escalation, not chronology. Preserve conversational meaning: what a statement answered matters as much as the statement.

**Pacing.** Keep conversational rhythm: the pause before an answer, reactions, laughter. Trim filler without flattening cadence - an over-cleaned speaker reads as scripted and loses credibility [E: ScriptCut]. In clips, get to the exchange fast; in long-form, let the conversation breathe.

**Visuals.** Reaction shots are content - cut to the listener during the statement when their face adds information [E: L-cut practice]. Cover compressions with cutaways or embrace clean jump cuts per the format's norms. Multi-camera: keep screen direction coherent.

**Audio.** Prioritize intelligibility across different mics/rooms; match levels between speakers; patch room tone. Never manufacture meaning: trimming is fine, Frankenbiting (stitching fragments into a sentence never said) crosses the ethical line [E: Becker/doc ethics].

**Common mistakes.** Ping-pong cutting on every line (never seeing anyone think); clipping the first syllable of responses; losing the question that makes the answer land; misleading rearrangement of answers.

**Do not port in.** Word-by-word karaoke captions over emotional exchanges; whooshes and stings.

## 3. Product demos / UGC / advertisements

### Case study: Dollar Shave Club launch video (2012)
A 93-second, ~$4,500 founder-led warehouse ad that drove 12,000 orders in 48 hours. Independent teardowns converge on the same reading [S: HookAds teardown; Conversion Sciences analysis]:
- The offer is sentence one, inside ~8 seconds ("For a dollar a month...") - clarity before comedy; the specific number ($1, not "affordable") creates the open loop.
- It names an enemy the audience already resents (feature-bloated $20 razor cartridges), then makes the incumbent look absurd - humor that punches up at a felt frustration, not random jokes.
- Founder-as-character builds trust the way a friend's recommendation does; the polish gap vs. Gillette's athlete-and-slow-motion-water ads *was* the point (Conversion Sciences reads the whole performance as "fed up-ness" - body language of a man on a mission).
- One CTA at the end, nothing else.
**What it proves / doesn't prove:** post-hoc teardowns by marketing blogs of one 2012 outlier, at a moment when founder-led deadpan was novel. It demonstrates offer-first structure and tonal contrast converting for a recurring-purchase commodity; it is not evidence the same tone still stands out in a feed full of imitators.

### Case study: Schiffer's smoothie commercial - craft from minimal kit
Daniel Schiffer's widely-shared home product commercial shows the shot-level mechanics: one LED panel and a foam-board bounce, products shot on a swivel chair for rotation, an unflattering white garage backdrop keyed to black, directional-blur keyframes faking whip transitions, a keyframed zoom re-timed with a mid-shot hold for text, and music + text added last [E: LensVid breakdown of the shoot/edit]. It demonstrates how little of the perceived production value is lighting or location - the perceived quality is created in the edit decisions.

**What the audience came for.** To see whether the product does the thing, from someone plausible. UGC works on perceived authenticity; polish beyond a point destroys it.

**Selection.** Proof first: the moment the product demonstrably works, the genuine reaction, the concrete result. Match demonstrations to claims: every stated benefit needs visible evidence [E/S; consistent with Kallaway's proof-first principle].

**Structure.** Problem or hook (the pain, the bold claim, the result shown early) -> demonstration as proof -> benefit framing -> close. Short ads: one claim, one proof, one call to action. Keep it truthful: persuasive sequencing is editorial work; misleading sequencing (implying results the demo does not show, hiding conditions) is both unethical and platform-risky.

**Pacing.** Fast but legible: the product must be identifiable in every demonstration shot; give the proof moment enough time to register (a result that flashes by convinces no one).

**Visuals.** Show the product clearly: lit, stable, large enough in frame; hands-in-use beats beauty shots for credibility. Dynamic locked-off footage comes from deliberate keyframed movement and masking, each move motivated (Schiffer's method) [E: Fstoppers]. Text callouts name the benefit at the moment of demonstration.

**Audio.** Product sound design is feel: the click, the pour, the snap - real, close, satisfying [E: Schiffer]. Music supports energy; voiceover/endorsement stays intelligible.

**Common mistakes.** Claim without proof in the same sequence; beauty shots of the product while the narration describes a use the viewer never sees; over-produced UGC that reads as an ad (killing its authenticity advantage); stock-looking B-roll under specific claims.

**Do not port in.** Slow documentary holds; ambiguous or ironic tone.

## 4. Tutorials / walkthroughs (how-to, software, process)

**What the audience came for.** To accomplish a task. Many scrub; many follow along in real time.

**Selection.** Every step that changes the state, in order; nothing else. Chronology is the structure - comprehension depends on sequence.

**Structure.** State the outcome up front ("by the end you'll have X"); then steps in order; recap only if long. Segment clearly so steps are findable (chapter-like structure serves the scrubbing viewer; segmenting also aids learning) [R: Mayer segmenting, ES 0.67].

**Pacing.** Slower than entertainment content; the viewer is processing and often mimicking. Pause after each completed action. Never cut away mid-action.

**Visuals.** Show the thing being operated, large and stable, at the moment it is described [R: temporal contiguity, ES 1.31]. Zoom to the relevant UI region while the action is performed; highlight/callout the exact element [R: signaling]. Keep cursor/pointer visible and deliberate.

**Audio.** Voice clarity above all; minimal music; interface sounds can stay if they confirm actions.

**Common mistakes.** Steps implied rather than shown ("then just configure it"); zooming after the action instead of before; music competing with instruction; intro padding before the first step.

**Do not port in.** Hook-first hype openings (state the outcome plainly); montage compression of steps.

## 5. Vlogs / lifestyle

**Case study: Casey Neistat's daily vlogs.** In Depth Cine's analysis identifies the repeatable mechanics [S: In Depth Cine video essay]: every episode has a deliberate arc (cold open with a hint of the payoff -> setup -> content -> wrap-up), with mini-arcs inside the day; whip-pan transitions are *shot with the edit in mind* (pan away at the end of one location, pan in at the next, cut on the blurred movement); jump cuts are used both as style and as a time-passing device (locked-off frame, reposition subject between takes); timelapses compress transitions; and crucially, the pace *drops* for long, uncut monologues when the content is heartfelt - the style serves the moment, not vice versa. The ethic underneath: "getting the shot matters more than perfect cinematography" - imperfections left in are what make it feel lived rather than produced. What this proves: a first-person analysis of one vlogger's publicly visible style; it documents the vocabulary, not its optimality.

**What the audience came for.** The person. Their day, their taste, their world. Content is personality-driven; polish serves the person, not the reverse.

**Selection.** Meaningful moments over coverage: the reaction, the mishap, the discovery, the quiet detail. The test: does this moment show who this person is or what this day felt like? Chronology is often the honest structure, but escalate within it - the day's best moment should not land where nobody is still watching.

**Structure.** Establish place early (arrival shots, location ambience); micro-arcs per scene (arrive, thing happens, beat of reaction/reflection); a loose day-arc (energy curve matching the day's events). Transitions between scenes are the craft: J/L cuts with location sound, match cuts, or clean hard cuts with ambience changes [E: Adobe/StudioBinder on split edits and bridges].

**Pacing.** Breathing rhythm: faster through logistics (travel, setup), slower in moments. Ambient sound and natural audio carry authenticity; do not bury them under music.

**Visuals.** Establishing shots at each new location; detail shots that show taste (the meal, the texture, the view); the person's face in moments of reaction. Refrain from over-producing: vlog credibility lives in slight roughness.

**Audio.** Ambient sound is content (the street, the kitchen, the weather). Music for travel/transition sequences and emotional underlining; drop it for intimate or comic moments.

**Common mistakes.** Montaging everything (a day with no moments, only motion); music wall-to-wall; cutting out the mundane beats that make the highlights land; location changes with no orientation.

**Do not port in.** Ad-style proof structure; sports-reel beat-sync.

## 6. Sports / highlights

### Case study: NFL Films / Steve Sabol
The Sabols invented most of the sports-highlight grammar now taken for granted: super slow-motion (a spinning football slowed until "we were watching something being born"), reverse angles, miked players, orchestral scores, mythic narration ("The autumn wind is a pirate"), and extreme close-ups - deliberately turning athletes into comic-book-scale figures [E: LA Times obituary/profile of Steve Sabol]. The documented principle: they filmed *around* the play (pre-game speeches, linemen in the trenches, idle moments, a cheerleader's hair flip) so the edit could build fable, not just recap. Al Michaels' summary is the transferable lesson: Sabol "combined art and journalism" - he "stripped away some of the mystery of the game and made it more interesting, rather than less." Slow motion here was conceptual, not decorative: it lets the audience inhabit a moment the live game never lets them see.

**What the audience came for.** Peak action, drama, and the feeling of the event - fast.

**Selection.** Highlights are the backbone: the plays that changed the game, the goals, the emotional reactions of players and fans [E: wearemob]. But preserve buildup and payoff: a goal without its build is a fact; with its build, it is drama. Where rights/footage allow, include the two or three seconds of developing play before the moment.

**Structure.** Strong start (a top-three moment in the first seconds), build, climax (the best moment last), brief reflection [E: wearemob + DOR's arc principle]. Recap/analysis formats follow narrative of the match instead.

**Pacing.** Matched to the event's energy: quick cuts convey intensity, longer holds build suspense [E: wearemob]. Slow motion and speed ramps for the critical moments (the goal, the catch) - sparingly, so they keep meaning [E: wearemob].

**Visuals.** Choose angles that show the play's shape (wide for tactics) and its emotion (tight for reactions). Scoreboards/timers/context graphics keep the viewer oriented and are persistent because they stay relevant [E: wearemob]. Avoid misleading chronology: do not reorder events to imply a comeback that did not happen (editorial integrity, sports audiences notice) [S].

**Audio.** The crowd is the emotion: boost it at peaks, duck it under commentary [E: wearemob]. Music for packages and reels; beat-sync is genre-normal here - sync the drops to the biggest moments, not every cut to every beat [E: DOR principle applied].

**Common mistakes.** Every play at the same intensity (no peaks); slow-mo on routine moments; cutting the reaction (the celebration IS the payoff); music fighting commentary.

**Do not port in.** Long ambient holds; interview-style cadence.

## 7. Gaming / esports clips

**What the audience came for.** Skill display, funny moments, and the streamer's personality - with high stimulus tolerance.

**Selection.** The 3-second test plus the share test: can you tell what's happening within 3 seconds, and does it make you want to show someone? If neither, cut it [E: DOR]. Ten strong clips beat thirty mixed ones: greedy inclusion drags the whole video [E: DOR].

**Structure.** Strong start (most impactful clip in the first 3-5 seconds), buildup, climax (flashiest play last) [E: DOR]. For stream-highlight compilations: alternate skill moments with personality moments (reactions, jokes) - the streamer's reactions are half the content.

**Pacing.** Vary cut lengths deliberately: break consecutive kills or fast fights into short cuts for speed; hold big plays (ace, pentakill) longer or add slow motion for contrast [E: DOR].

**Visuals.** Keep the game readable: do not crop out the UI elements that explain the play (health, minimap, ability state) unless the moment is pure reaction. Zooms onto the decisive detail are motivated here (the pixel-perfect shot). Captions and meme inserts are genre-normal - they are the humor layer, not decoration [E: eklipse/gaming practice].

**Audio.** Match the strongest scene to the track's drop; fast-tempo tracks for fight sections, something with resonance for the finish [E: DOR]. Keep the streamer's voice and game audio where they carry the moment (the clutch call-out, the team scream). Copyright note: flagged music is a real platform risk for this genre [E: DOR].

**Common mistakes.** Including mediocre clips because they happened on stream; uniform cut lengths (monotony at high speed); cutting before the play resolves; losing the reaction that made the clip worth clipping.

**Do not port in.** Documentary restraint; corporate polish.

## 8. Cinematic montage

### Case study: Baby Driver - when music is the master clock
Editor Paul Machliss describes the extreme end of music-led cutting: all 29 soundtrack songs were chosen before the shoot; the team built a 100-minute "radio play" (dialogue + music + temp effects) years before filming, then full animatics; Machliss edited on set from video assist on ~95% of shooting days because every action beat had to land on a predetermined musical moment [E: Motion Picture Association + Televisual interviews with Machliss]. The hard constraint they adopted: *do not edit the music* - because pulling two seconds from a shot makes every later beat drift off the song, and "you can't just take two seconds of music out." When a scene ran long, the solution was narrative (Baby rewinds the track on his iPod), not a music edit. What this proves: a choreographed feature film is the special case where the "cut on the beat" instinct must be absolute, and it shows the true cost of that choice - the picture must serve the score's rigid grid, with enormous preproduction. It is the counterexample that clarifies the general short-form guidance (beat-sync for emphasis, not as a constant): constant beat-lock is a full-production commitment, not a post-hoc setting.

**What the audience came for.** Feeling: awe, energy, melancholy, triumph. The montage communicates by collision and progression, not explanation.

**Selection.** Images that escalate a feeling or idea, chosen for their relationships (rhyme, contrast, progression), not their individual beauty alone. Each adjacent pair should produce something together [E/theory: Eisenstein].

**Structure.** An arc in one sentence ("chaos to control," "alone to together") that the shot order enacts. Know the levers: metric (length-driven pulse), rhythmic (movement-matched), tonal (emotion-matched), intellectual (idea collision) [theory: Eisenstein's methods]. Most modern montage is rhythmic + tonal with a clear escalation.

**Pacing.** The montage is where beat-alignment is most legitimate - but sync moments (drops, hits, phrase turns) rather than every beat, and let movement within shots drive cut points (rhythmic montage) rather than a metronome (metric-only cutting reads as mechanical) [E: PowerEdit/Steed caveats applied to the genre that most invites the exception].

**Visuals.** Match on movement, shape, or color across cuts; vary scale progressively (wide -> close as intensity rises); hold the final image.

**Audio.** Music leads here - this is the format where track-first workflows fit [E: Hillier-Smith music-first]. Sound design (risers, impacts, atmosphere) marks the structural turns. Silence or near-silence before the final beat multiplies it.

**Common mistakes.** Uniform shot lengths with no arc; images that are individually strong but relationally random; effects density that hides the images; an ending that fades instead of lands.

**Do not port in.** Caption-heavy treatment; voiceover-driven structure (unless the montage scores a narration by design).

## Cross-type matrix: what changes, what does not

| Decision | Universal rule | Format-weighted default |
|---|---|---|
| Cut density | Waves, not metronome | Tutorial/vlog slow; sports/gaming fast; educational medium |
| Beat-sync | Deliberate, moment-level | Rare: tutorial, interview. Normal: sports, gaming, montage |
| Captions | Readable, synced, off the subject | Minimal: tutorial. Standard: educational, UGC. Heavy/meme: gaming |
| B-roll role | Evidence, cover, orient | Evidence: educational, product. Cover: interview. Content itself: montage |
| Reaction shots | Content, hold them | Critical: podcast, gaming, vlog. Rare: tutorial |
| Music role | Serves scene emotion | Support: educational, interview. Driver: montage, sports, gaming. Optional: tutorial |
| Chronology | Escalation over timeline | Keep chronology: tutorial, process. Break it: highlights, ads, docs |
