# Editing Review: Evaluating a Finished First Cut

How a skilled editor evaluates a completed assembly and what they do about what they find. Use this to review your own first cut before presenting it, and to structure revision passes.

**No numerical quality formula exists, and this document does not invent one.** Review is organized observation, not scoring. The output of a review is a list of specific, located, actionable observations.

---

## Part 1: The three tiers of findings

Separate every observation into one tier before acting on it:

### Tier 1: Objective defects - always fix
Detectable without taste, verifiable by replay:
- Black frames, frozen frames, unintended repeated frames
- Clipped or distorted audio; dialogue cut mid-word or mid-breath; missing audio
- Broken A/V synchronization (lips off sound, effect off action)
- Captions overlapping each other, exceeding safe areas, exceeding readable speed (BBC: 160-180 wpm recommended; ~0.3s/word minimum display), broken timing [R/standard: BBC]
- Unmatched color/exposure between shots meant to match; wrong aspect or framing errors (decapitated subject, product out of frame)
- Music or effects masking speech; audible room-tone holes at cuts
- Platform violations: wrong duration, wrong aspect, unreadable text at delivery size

### Tier 2: Editorial concerns - fix when the brief supports it
Judgment calls grounded in this KB; phrase them with evidence:
- Weak or misleading opening (does not confirm the packaging's promise)
- Story spine unclear; middle sags; loops opened and never closed
- Pacing flat where it should wave; a section that repeats the same energy or information
- Unmotivated B-roll (does not answer the narration); missing reaction shots
- Music contradicting the scene's emotion; beat-synced cutting where underscore should drive
- Unnecessary effects; over-trimmed speech that reads as scripted; emotional beats cut too tight
- Structural mismatch with the content type (montage pacing in an interview)

### Tier 3: Subjective preferences - defer to style
Not defects; do not "fix" them against the creator's style:
- Minimalist vs. energetic feel; typography choices within legibility limits; music taste within emotional fit; humor density; transition aesthetics
If two skilled editors could disagree, it lives here. Sclip's style-learning system owns it (see `editorial-taste.md` Part 5).

## Part 2: The review process

Professional review habits, adapted:

1. **Watch the whole cut at full speed, start to finish, before touching anything.** Rhythm and pacing judgments are only valid at playback speed; slow review masks drift and flattens feel [E: ToolsForFilm]. Note moments by timecode as they snag - do not stop to fix.
2. **Catalog first responses.** Editors record their immediate reactions to material because memory fails over a long project; your first-pass reactions approximate the audience's [E: Frame.io]. Keep the first-watch notes separate from analysis notes.
3. **Watch with sound only (eyes closed).** Exposes dialogue-flow problems, room-tone holes, abrupt audio transitions, music-vs-meaning conflicts that picture hides.
4. **Watch with picture only (muted).** Exposes visual rhythm problems, unmotivated cutaways, caption collisions, hierarchy failures that audio smooths over.
5. **Watch on a different screen.** A different monitor/TV/phone reveals color, level, and readability issues invisible on the editing monitor; also simulates the real viewing context [E: PremiumBeat QC practice]. For social deliverables, the phone is the reference screen.
6. **Get fresh eyes when the stakes justify it.** A fresh viewer reactivates critical evaluation - you instantly spot the bumps, pauses, drags, and confusions you went blind to [E: Frame.io]. For Hermes: a separate review pass (or model) with no knowledge of the edit's intentions is the equivalent.
7. **Stage the polish level to the review stage.** Early cuts shown for feedback should be "ugly but watchable": levels roughed in, obvious gaffes fixed, transitions and score sketched - polished enough that reviewers react to the edit, not to technical noise; rough enough that big changes are still cheap and the editor is not yet defensive [E: Film Editing Pro].
8. **Watch someone watch it.** MrBeast's documented review practice is to sit ~10 fans/friends in front of the cut and watch *them*, marking exactly where they get bored or reach for their phones, then rework or cut those moments [E: as described in Aprilynne Alter's analysis of his interviews]. The audience member is a live retention instrument - more sensitive than the editor's own fresh eyes. For Hermes: the analytics equivalent is drop-off data per moment; the process equivalent is any proxy that measures attention, not intention.

## Part 3: The review rubric (area by area)

For each area: the questions to answer and the evidence to check. Answer with located observations (timecode + what + why), never with vibes.

### Opening clarity
- Read the source immediately before and after the actual selected opening. Does the edit establish enough context without omitted speech? If not, extend backward, choose a self-contained passage, or restructure. This checks semantic context, not a compulsory viral hook.
- Can a cold viewer state, within 5 seconds (short-form) or 30 seconds (long-form), what this is and why to stay?
- Does the opening confirm the promise made by the title/thumbnail/first frame? [E: Kallaway lock-in zone]
- Is any orientation missing (who, where, what is at stake)? Is any orientation repeated that the packaging already delivered?

### Narrative coherence
- Can you state the spine in 2-3 sentences? Does every clip serve it?
- Is there always an open question? Do all opened loops close?
- Does the order escalate, or is it the shooting order? Does chronology carry comprehension anywhere (process, cause-effect) - and is it preserved there?

### Information completeness
- Does every claim have its proof or context within the video? Is anything referenced that the viewer never sees or hears?
- Is anything explained twice? Is anything explained zero times that needed once?
- Would the target viewer (per the brief) understand every term at first use?

### Pacing
- Graph section energies: waves or flatline? Does the middle renew attention (new information, visual, question, or feeling every 20-60 seconds)?
- Are emotional beats given more time than informational ones? Any moment that outstays its delivery?
- Where does it end relative to the final peak? How much tail follows the payoff?

### Visual continuity
- Screen direction consistent within scenes? Axis crosses intended?
- Jump cuts confident (on thought boundaries) or glitchy (mid-phrase)? Near-identical adjacent framings?
- Cut points on motion where motion exists? Scale progression matching intensity?

### Audio continuity
- Room tone consistent across cuts? Ambience matching the location?
- Music edits musical (on phrase boundaries, not mid-phrase stumbles)? Audio transitions matching the emotional join (soft for continuation, hard for rupture)?
- Dialogue rhythm natural - breaths and pauses that carry meaning preserved?

### Emotional effect
- Name the intended feeling per section. Does the cut produce it? Does the music agree with it? [R: film-music research]
- Are reactions held? Do jokes, revelations, and payoffs get their beat of air?
- Is any moment manipulated (fake tension, unearned sentiment)?

### Captions and typography
- Readable at delivery size on the target platform? Within speed and duration limits [R/standard: BBC]?
- Positioned clear of faces, product, action, and platform UI? One thing to read at a time?
- Emphasis text sparse enough to still signal? Does text exit when its moment passes?

### Appropriate use of effects
- Every zoom, transition, sting, and graphic justified in one line? Count elements per minute and compare to the content type's norms.
- Does the piece survive with effects muted? (If not, the story is weak [E: Steed].)

### Visuals-speech relationship
- Does the picture show the thing being named, at the moment it is named (or deliberately withhold it)? [R: temporal contiguity]
- Is any B-roll arbitrary wallpaper? Is any spoken abstraction missing its visual evidence?

### Alignment with the brief
- Same video as the brief: audience, length, tone, platform, goal? Drift between sections?
- Anything in the cut the brief's owner would not want said or shown (claims, people, moments)?

## Part 4: Interpreting feedback (when humans review)

When a director, client, or test viewer responds to the cut:

- **Ask open questions, not leading ones.** "Was there anything you found distracting?" "What did you think the message was?" "How do you think the character feels?" - not "did you like it?" [E: Film Editing Pro].
- **Diagnose the problem behind the suggested solution.** Feedback often arrives as a fix ("make it faster") that identifies a real problem (a sagging middle) with a wrong prescription. Find what made them feel it before changing anything [E: Film Editing Pro].
- **Sort before acting.** Some notes are misunderstandings caused by rough-cut artifacts (missing music, temp color); some are taste; some are real. Address real problems; explain artifact-driven confusion; park taste disagreements for the style owner.
- **Allow time before re-cutting.** Emotional attachment to the first assembly distorts judgment immediately after feedback; distance restores objectivity [E: Film Editing Pro].
- **Beware the over-cut polish pass.** Late-stage "cleaning up" can remove the idiosyncrasies that gave the piece its heart [E: Mary Lampson via Bricca].

## Part 5: Example observations (format for feedback to Hermes)

Good review output is located, specific, and actionable. Weak vs. strong:

- Weak: "The pacing is off."
  Strong: "0:41-1:10 - five consecutive talking-head shots at the same framing and energy after the demo. Middle sags here. Insert the screen recording of step 2 (exists at source 04:12) and cut the repeated claim at 0:58."

- Weak: "The music doesn't work."
  Strong: "0:05-0:30 - upbeat track under the founder describing the company's near-collapse. Music contradicts the scene's emotion (film-music research: viewers read the scene through the track). Suggest sparse piano or no music until the turn at 0:31."

- Weak: "Too many effects."
  Strong: "0:12, 0:19, 0:26 - three punch-in zooms at regular intervals with no change in content importance. Reads as automated. Remove two; keep the zoom at 0:19 where the speaker reveals the number."

- Weak: "Bad captions."
  Strong: "0:33 - caption covers the product being demonstrated. 0:47 - caption at ~230 wpm, unreadable at full speed (BBC guidance: 160-180 wpm). Reposition and retime."

- Weak: "Hook could be better."
  Strong: "0:00-0:04 - opens on logo animation. The result moment at source 01:52 ('it shipped, 40,000 users') is the stronger opening: it confirms the title's promise immediately. Swap."
