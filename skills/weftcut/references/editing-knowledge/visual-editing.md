# Visual Editing: Shots, Framing, Motion, B-roll, Text, Transitions

---

## Part 1: Shot selection

### Principle: Select the moment that carries the meaning, not the moment that looks best
**Purpose.** Every shot in the cut must justify itself by what it does for the story or the feeling.
**When relevant.** The selects pass on any project.
**Evidence to examine.** What does this shot contribute that no other shot does: information, proof, emotion, orientation, rhythm?
**Good decision.** In interviews: select the sentence that carries the idea, not the whole answer around it [E: ScriptCut]. In highlights: the playable test - can you tell what is happening within 3 seconds, and does it make you want to show someone? If neither, cut it [E: DOR gaming criteria]. In documentary: prefer footage closest to firsthand experience; Bricca's "hierarchy of experience" ranks direct observation of events above people describing events secondhand (Aaron Wickenden: the closer to the event itself, the more interesting the footage) [E: Documentary Editing].
**Potential mistake.** Keeping a shot because it was expensive to get, is technically beautiful, or is the creator's favorite - when a plainer shot tells the story better. Also: keeping whole answers "for context" when context belongs in the structure, not inside one overlong clip [E: ScriptCut].
**Exceptions.** Atmosphere shots that establish place and mood carry orientation value even with no "information."
**How to evaluate.** For each shot, one line: "this shot is here because ___." If the blank is "it looks nice" or "we had it," interrogate harder.

### Principle: Reactions are content
**Purpose.** What happens on a face after an event is often the event, for the viewer.
**When relevant.** Dialogue, interviews, podcasts, reactions, comedy, any human moment.
**Evidence to examine.** What does the listener's/reactor's face do during and after the key statement?
**Good decision.** Hold the reaction after the line; use an L-cut so we hear the next line while still watching the reaction land [E: Adobe on L-cuts in dialogue]. Choose reaction shots that add information (surprise, doubt, suppressed laughter), not neutral coverage.
**Potential mistake.** Cutting mechanically between speakers on each line ("ping-pong" coverage) so the viewer never sees anyone process anything.
**Exceptions.** Fast comedy sometimes plays everything on the speaker for timing reasons.
**How to evaluate.** For each key statement: whose face tells the more interesting story during it - the speaker's or the listener's? Cut accordingly.

## Part 2: Framing and scale

### Principle: Shot scale is an information and intimacy dial
**Purpose.** Wide shots orient; medium shots inform; close-ups feel.
**When relevant.** Selecting which angle or crop of a moment to use; deciding when to reframe.
**Evidence to examine.** What does the viewer need right now: where are we (wide), what is happening (medium), or what is felt (close)?
**Good decision.** Move from wide to close as the scene's emotional or informational focus narrows ("hawkeye" broad-to-deep is the short-form echo of classic coverage logic) [E: Kallaway; classical craft]. Research note: viewers process cuts to closer views more fluently than cuts to wider views, which fits the natural pattern of attention zooming in [R: Frontiers 2023].
**Potential mistake.** Staying wide during the emotional peak; or opening tight with no orientation and confusing the geography.
**Exceptions.** Withholding the close-up during a peak can be a deliberate distance device (grief observed from afar).
**How to evaluate.** Map shot scale against the story's intensity curve: scale should generally tighten as intensity rises.

### Principle: Cut on movement, not against it
**Purpose.** Motion masks cuts and carries the eye.
**When relevant.** Choosing exact cut points in any footage with movement.
**Evidence to examine.** Where in the action does a gesture, walk, pan, or object movement peak or complete?
**Good decision.** Cut during movement (match-on-action): viewers miss roughly a third of such cuts, versus about a tenth between static shots [R: Smith et al. 2012]. For repeated actions across takes, match the movement phase.
**Potential mistake.** Cutting just before a movement completes (frustrating) or during dead stillness when a movement was available frames away (visible cut).
**Exceptions.** Static, formal interviews cut on speech rhythm instead; the cut lands on prosodic beats, not motion.
**How to evaluate.** Scrub each cut point: is something in motion through the cut, or did you cut in dead air between actions?

## Part 3: Continuity grammar

### Principle: Keep screen direction coherent
**Purpose.** The viewer builds an implicit map; crossing it without reorientation disorients measurably [R: Frontiers 2023 on 180-degree violations].
**When relevant.** Multi-angle scenes, interviews, dialogue, any spatially consistent action.
**Evidence to examine.** Which way do subjects face and move across adjacent shots?
**Good decision.** Keep the axis of action; if you must cross it, reorient with a wide shot, a camera move, or a neutral (head-on) shot.
**Potential mistake.** Answer shots where both speakers face the same screen direction; a subject walking off left re-entering from left.
**Exceptions.** Music videos and montage freely ignore direction for energy; chaotic sequences cross deliberately.
**How to evaluate.** Draw the axis mentally for each scene; list any crosses and confirm each was intended.

### Principle: Jump cuts are a style choice, not an accident to half-hide
**Purpose.** Time compression inside a continuous shot must read as intentional.
**When relevant.** Talking-head, vlogs, any single-camera speech.
**Evidence to examine.** Does the jump cut land on a thought boundary? Is the jump visible and confident, or timid?
**Good decision.** Cut cleanly at phrase boundaries so the jump reads as rhythm; or hide the compression behind a motivated cutaway when invisibility serves better [E: editorcuts B-roll mechanics].
**Potential mistake.** The "almost-jump": trimming so little that the head twitches mid-phrase - reads as a glitch, the worst of both worlds.
**Exceptions.** In long-form interview and documentary, visible jump cuts on people can feel disrespectful or cheap; cover with cutaways instead.
**How to evaluate.** Play each compression: intentional rhythm or visible glitch? Consistent with the rest of the piece?

### Principle: The 30-degree guideline is a guideline
**Purpose.** Adjacent shots of the same subject should differ meaningfully in angle or size, or the cut reads as a jump.
**When relevant.** Multi-camera or multi-take coverage.
**Evidence to examine.** Do adjacent shots of the same subject differ by roughly 30+ degrees of angle or a clear change of shot size?
**Good decision.** Pair wide/medium, medium/close, or angles separated by real distance.
**Potential mistake.** Cutting between near-identical framings (same angle, slightly different size) - a stutter, not a cut.
**Exceptions.** Jump-cut style deliberately violates this for rhythm.
**How to evaluate.** Thumbnail the adjacent shots side by side: obviously different, or confusingly similar?

## Part 4: Movement, zooms, reframes

### Principle: Digital movement must have a motivation and a terminus
**Purpose.** Punch-ins, zooms, and animated reframes redirect attention; unmotivated movement is noise.
**When relevant.** Adding keyframed scale/position moves to static footage (standard in short-form and product work).
**Evidence to examine.** What should the viewer look at more closely, and when do they stop needing to?
**Good decision.** Zoom to redirect attention toward an important detail or emotional reaction, arriving with the narration that names it [E/S; consistent with signaling research: cues that highlight essential material improve comprehension, R: Mayer]. Schiffer's product work adds keyframed movement deliberately, with masks and tracked motion, to make locked-off footage feel directed - each move answers "why this movement, here" [E: Fstoppers on Schiffer].
**Potential mistake.** The metronome zoom: a punch-in every N seconds "to keep it dynamic." This is the canonical AI-looking failure (see `common-ai-editing-failures.md`).
**Exceptions.** Some creators' signature styles include frequent punch-ins; that belongs to the style-learning system, not to general quality.
**How to evaluate.** For each move: what does the viewer understand or feel because of it? Does the move settle (arrive and hold) or drift?

### Principle: Reframe for the platform, not around the subject
**Purpose.** Vertical crops of horizontal footage must preserve the hierarchy of the original frame.
**When relevant.** 16:9 source delivered as 9:16, or any crop.
**Evidence to examine.** Where is the subject's face, the action, the text safe zone? Do platform UI overlays (caption zones, buttons) cover critical content?
**Good decision.** Track the crop to the action; keep captions inside the safe zone; verify the product or face is never clipped mid-gesture.
**Potential mistake.** A static center crop that decapitates the subject when they lean, or hides the product being demonstrated at frame edge.
**Exceptions.** Symmetric, centered content crops trivially.
**How to evaluate.** Watch the reframed cut on the actual platform aspect: does anything important leave the frame or sit under UI?

### Principle: Always fill the entire screen (Full Canvas Coverage)
**Purpose.** Video must completely cover the composition canvas; empty black bars or a small video floating in a corner look unprofessional and broken.
**When relevant.** Every video layer placed on the timeline across all aspect ratios (especially 16:9 footage in vertical 9:16 Shorts/Reels).
**Evidence to examine.** Does the video layer's scaled dimensions cover 100% of the canvas width and height? Is there black space on any border or corner?
**Good decision.** Always use `calculate_video_fit` with `mode: 'cover'` to scale and position the clip so 100% of the screen is filled edge-to-edge. Supply focal points (`focus_x`, `focus_y`) based on `vision_inspect` evidence to keep subjects centered.
**Potential mistake.** Leaving a video clip unscaled (1.0x) or shrunk into a corner/top-right, leaving vast black voids across the preview.
**Exceptions.** Picture-in-picture (PIP), insets, or multi-box splits ONLY when explicitly requested by the user.
**How to evaluate.** Background reviewer automatically audits `video_coverage`; verify composed frames with `vision_inspect` to ensure zero unintended black bars.

## Part 5: B-roll and cutaways

### Principle: B-roll is evidence, not wallpaper
**Purpose.** A cutaway should show the thing being said, hide an edit, orient the viewer, or shift emphasis - otherwise it should not be there.
**When relevant.** Every proposed cutaway.
**Evidence to examine.** What is said at this moment, and which shot answers it? Editorcuts: the best cutaway answers the narration; a generic office shot under "we found the fault during final inspection" fails, a close-up of the actual defect succeeds [E].
**Good decision.** Build the A-roll edit first, then place cutaways where they solve a specific problem: a jump cut to hide, a claim to prove, a place to establish, a detail to show, or a purposeful visual break [E: editorcuts]. Show the item when it is named (temporal contiguity improves comprehension, ES 1.31) [R: Mayer].
**Potential mistake.** B-roll on a timer ("we haven't shown B-roll in 8 seconds") producing stock-footage wallpaper; or cutting away from a speaker on every sentence so a human moment becomes voiceover under generic footage (editorcuts: constant B-roll reduces a person to background audio) [E].
**Exceptions.** Montage sequences are built from B-roll by design - but there each shot is the content, judged on rhythm and progression.
**How to evaluate.** For each cutaway, name its job (evidence / cover / orient / emphasize / breathe). Watch the cutaway segment muted: does the picture still connect to the story?

### Principle: Coverage has levels; collect and use all of them
**Purpose.** A story needs establishing, action, and detail shots; they are not interchangeable.
**When relevant.** Planning or selecting B-roll for a scene.
**Evidence to examine.** Do you have an establishing shot (where), medium action (what), and inserts/details (the specific thing)?
**Good decision.** Wide to orient, medium to show the work, close/insert to show the evidence - in the order the narration needs them [E: editorcuts].
**Potential mistake.** Three near-identical close-ups and no establishing shot: the viewer never learns where they are.
**Exceptions.** In fast short-form, orientation can be carried by a text label or a half-second wide; do not over-index on establishing shots at the cost of pace.
**How to evaluate.** List the cutaways by level. If one level is missing, was that a choice?

### Principle: Duration is pacing, not a rule
**Purpose.** How long a cutaway holds depends on what it must communicate and the pace around it.
**When relevant.** Trimming every cutaway.
**Evidence to examine.** Has the viewer received what this shot communicates? Is there an unfolding action inside the shot?
**Good decision.** Common working ranges: most cutaways 2-5 seconds; faster in short-form; longer (5-10s) only when the shot carries observation, atmosphere, or emotional weight [E: editorcuts, citing professional guidance - treat as defaults, not rules]. Cut when the shot has delivered; hold when something is still unfolding.
**Potential mistake.** Trimming every cutaway to the same length; or holding a static shot after it has been read.
**Exceptions.** A deliberately long hold on a detail can be an emphasis device.
**How to evaluate.** Per cutaway: at what frame has a typical viewer "read" it? Is there a reason to stay longer?

### Principle: B-roll must match the scene technically
**Purpose.** Mismatched footage announces the edit instead of serving it.
**When relevant.** Any inserted footage from a different source, day, or camera.
**Evidence to examine.** Frame rate, exposure, white balance, color treatment, grain/sharpness, lens character vs. the A-roll.
**Good decision.** Match or grade to match; if it cannot match, make the difference look intentional (a stylized insert, archival treatment) [E: editorcuts style-drift failure].
**Potential mistake.** Stock footage with a different color signature dropped into a personal vlog: instant "bolt-on" feel.
**Exceptions.** Clearly signaled inserts (screen recordings, archival, meme inserts) are understood as different material.
**How to evaluate.** Play the transition into and out of the cutaway: does the footage feel like the same world?

## Part 6: Text, captions, graphics

### Principle: Captions are for comprehension first
**Purpose.** Text on screen must be readable, timed to speech, and never fight the picture.
**When relevant.** Any caption or subtitle pass.
**Evidence to examine.** Reading speed, line length, position, timing, contrast.
**Good decision.** BBC subtitle guidelines (professional broadcast standard): recommended speed 160-180 words per minute; minimum display roughly 0.3 seconds per word (e.g., ~1.2s for 4 words); maximum two lines; break lines at linguistically coherent points (keep phrases together, never break mid-word); position to avoid covering critical picture information; prioritize editing and timing over line-break aesthetics [R/standard: BBC]. Short-form word-by-word caption styles trade some readability for energy: legitimate as a style, but the words must still be sync-accurate and on screen long enough to register.
**Potential mistake.** Captions that lag or lead speech; karaoke captions covering the speaker's mouth; text that flashes too fast to read; captions over B-roll text or busy backgrounds with no contrast treatment.
**Exceptions.** Sound-off-first platforms justify more caption presence; artistic text (kinetic typography) is a design element, judged as design.
**How to evaluate.** Watch once reading only the captions (are they complete, correctly timed, unbroken?), once ignoring them (do they pull you off the picture against your will?). Check against the safe zones.

### Principle: Emphasis text is a signal, used sparingly
**Purpose.** On-screen keywords and callouts highlight the essential; overused, they become noise.
**When relevant.** Educational and short-form content especially.
**Evidence to examine.** Which words genuinely carry the point? Mayer's signaling principle: cues that highlight essential material improve learning (ES 0.70), but effects weaken when signals are overused [R: Mayer, boundary conditions].
**Good decision.** A few words that back up what is being said, timed with the speech (Film Booth: say the point, show the point as a few words of text, at the same time) [E]. Place labels next to the thing they name, not in a distant corner [R: spatial contiguity].
**Potential mistake.** Full-sentence text duplicating the narration (redundancy principle: identical printed + spoken text can hurt processing; short reworded phrases are the safer pattern) [R: Mayer, with boundary conditions].
**Exceptions.** Meme and commentary formats use dense text as part of the aesthetic.
**How to evaluate.** Count concurrent text elements: is there ever more than one thing to read at once? Does each text element exit when its moment passes?

### Principle: Graphics enter with a reason and leave with one
**Purpose.** Lower thirds, callouts, charts, and animations are claims on attention; they must earn their screen time.
**When relevant.** Any graphic element.
**Evidence to examine.** What does the graphic add that picture and speech do not? When has it finished?
**Good decision.** Introduce as the relevant information arrives; remove when the topic moves on. Sports context graphics (score, clock, player name) persist because they remain relevant [E: wearemob].
**Potential mistake.** A lower third that stays for the whole segment; a chart that appears after the narration described it; decorative motion graphics looping behind content.
**Exceptions.** Persistent brand elements (a subtle watermark) are a business choice, not an editorial one - keep them out of the action zones.
**How to evaluate.** For each graphic: entrance cue and exit cue exist and are motivated.

## Part 7: Transitions

### Principle: The hard cut is the default; every other transition is a choice
**Purpose.** Transitions carry meaning (time passing, place changing, tone shifting); a decorative transition spends that meaning on nothing.
**When relevant.** Every shot boundary.
**Evidence to examine.** What relationship between these two shots should the viewer feel?
**Good decision.** Hard cut for continuity and energy; dissolve for time passage or soft connection; fade for beginnings/endings; J/L cuts (audio-led) for flow between scenes [E: Adobe, StudioBinder]. Flashy transitions (whips, glitch, spins) when the content's energy genuinely calls for them - some sports and gaming work qualifies.
**Potential mistake.** A library transition between every clip ("transition pack" editing), which both dates the work and dilutes the moments that deserved a real transition.
**Exceptions.** Style-driven content (certain vlog and meme aesthetics) uses transitions as signature - style-system territory.
**How to evaluate.** List non-cut transitions and justify each in a word (time, place, tone, energy). Any without a justification becomes a cut.

### Principle: Match cuts are the strongest transition because they carry meaning
**Purpose.** Matching shape, movement, color, or idea across a cut makes the transition itself say something.
**When relevant.** Scene changes, time jumps, thematic links.
**Evidence to examine.** Is there a visual or conceptual rhyme between the end of one shot and the start of the next?
**Good decision.** Kallaway's "visual matching": align what is seen with what is said so comprehension is effortless [E]. Classic match-on-shape or match-on-movement for scene changes.
**Potential mistake.** A match cut that is technically clever but connects nothing (form without meaning).
**Exceptions.** -
**How to evaluate.** What does the match communicate? If the answer is "it looks cool," weigh it against the cost.

## Part 8: Montage

### Principle: Montage is meaning through collision
**Purpose.** A montage compresses time, process, or feeling by colliding shots; the juxtaposition is the content.
**When relevant.** Time compression, emotional progression, hype sequences, cinematic interludes.
**Evidence to examine.** What should the sequence make the viewer feel or understand that no single shot could?
**Good decision.** Know the levers (Eisenstein's methods, film theory [R-adjacent/theory]): metric (cut by length - pure pulse), rhythmic (cut by movement within shots), tonal (cut by emotional tone of images), overtonal (all combined), intellectual (collision of images to produce an idea). Modern montage usually blends rhythmic + tonal: movement-matched cuts whose images escalate in emotional charge.
**Potential mistake.** A montage that is just "clips we didn't use" at uniform length over music: no progression, no collision, no point.
**Exceptions.** -
**How to evaluate.** Describe the montage's arc in one sentence (e.g., "chaos to control"). Does the shot order actually escalate? Does each adjacent pair produce something together?

## Part 9: Color and effects (editorial view)

### Principle: Grade and effects serve the scene's intention
**Purpose.** Color and visual effects set tone and direct attention; they are not a polish layer applied uniformly.
**When relevant.** Any grading or VFX decision.
**Evidence to examine.** What should this scene feel like? Does the treatment match the footage's purpose (documentary credibility vs. commercial sheen)?
**Good decision.** Consistency within a scene; deliberate contrast between scenes when the story changes register (sports: enhance team colors, or add warmth for nostalgia - in service of the storyline) [E: wearemob].
**Potential mistake.** A heavy stylized LUT on testimonial footage (credibility damage); effects that obscure the product or the face.
**Exceptions.** Strong stylization is the point in some music and fashion work.
**How to evaluate.** Does the treatment help the viewer read the scene's tone correctly? Does any effect hide the thing the shot exists to show?
