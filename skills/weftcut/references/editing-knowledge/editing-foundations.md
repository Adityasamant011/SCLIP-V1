# Editing Foundations: Universal Principles

These ten principles apply across content types and styles. They are not equally weighted. When they conflict, Walter Murch's priority order is the working tiebreaker among professional editors: **emotion first, then story, then rhythm, then eye-trace, then two-dimensional screen space, then three-dimensional space** [E]. Murch published this as the "Rule of Six" with percentage weights (emotion 51%, story 23%, rhythm 10%, eye-trace 7%, 2D plane 5%, 3D space 4%), but his own text presents it as a checklist of priorities, not arithmetic. Treat it as: sacrifice lower priorities before higher ones. Never score a cut numerically [E, with caveat].

---

## 1. Attention

**What it means.** The edit controls where the viewer looks and what they notice, moment to moment. Viewers do not watch the whole frame; they watch what the edit points at.

**Why it matters.** Every downstream goal (comprehension, emotion, retention) depends on the viewer having noticed the thing that matters. A fact spoken while the viewer's eyes are elsewhere is a fact lost.

**Evidence.**
- Viewers' eye movements synchronize strongly while watching film, and professional editing increases that synchrony: viewers look at the same place at the same time. Attention is steerable [R: Smith, Levin & Cutting 2012].
- Cuts placed during motion are partially masked: in experiments, viewers missed roughly one third of cuts that occurred during a coherent action, versus about one in ten cuts between static scenes. Motion hides the cut and carries attention across it [R: Smith et al. 2012].
- Cuts to a *closer* view of the same scene (scale-in) are noticed less and processed more fluently than cuts to a wider view (scale-out), in EEG response studies. The brain treats moving closer as natural attention behavior [R: Frontiers in Neuroscience 2023].
- Cues that highlight essential material (outlines, headings, emphasis, arrows, visual spotlighting) improve learning from multimedia, median effect size 0.70 across 26 of 28 experiments [R: Mayer, signaling principle].
- The continuity editing rules themselves are attention engineering. Smith's Attentional Theory of Cinematic Continuity: a cut produces continuity when attentional cues before the cut (motion, a character's gaze, an offscreen sound, a conversational turn) create an expectation that the next shot fulfills. Match-action, shot/reverse-shot, the 180-degree rule and point-of-view cutting all work through this mechanism, not through a spatial map in the viewer's head [R: Smith 2012, Projections].

**How a professional applies it.** Editors design the "eye-trace": they know where the viewer is looking at the end of a shot and choose the next shot so attention lands where the story needs it. Kallaway's "visual matching" is the short-form version: what is on screen at each moment matches and reinforces what is being said, so the viewer never has to choose between listening and looking [E].

**When it might NOT apply.** Deliberate disorientation (a chaotic sequence meant to overwhelm) trades attention control for feeling. Ambient, slow content (a landscape hold, a quiet documentary moment) intentionally relaxes attention direction so the viewer can wander inside the frame.

**How Hermes could apply it.** Before each cut, estimate: where is the viewer looking now, and where will the first frame of the next shot put their eyes? Prefer cutaways whose subject sits where attention already is, or that deliberately move it for a reason. When the narration names a thing, the frame should show the thing (or be withholding it on purpose).

**What failure looks like.** The narration says "the third setting" while the screen shows an unrelated wide shot; B-roll with a strong motion magnet (a face, a bright moving object) pulling eyes away from the information; captions placed where they fight the subject for the same eye-time.

## 2. Clarity

**What it means.** The viewer can follow what is happening and what is being claimed without working harder than the content requires. Kallaway calls the extreme version "comprehension maxing": engineer every second so the widest possible audience understands it on first pass [E].

**Why it matters.** Cognitive capacity is finite. Every unit spent decoding the edit is a unit not spent on the message. People learn better when extraneous material is removed (coherence principle: supported in 18 of 19 experiments, median effect size 0.86), when related words and pictures appear together in space (spatial contiguity: 9 of 9, ES 0.82) and time (temporal contiguity: 8 of 8, ES 1.31) [R: Mayer].

**Evidence.**
- Removing interesting-but-irrelevant material improves learning; seductive details measurably hurt it [R: Mayer coherence principle].
- Showing a graphic at the same moment its narration explains it beats showing it before or after [R: Mayer temporal contiguity].
- Making viewers feel smart retains them; making them feel dumb loses them. Film Booth: the biggest mistake educational creators make is showing how smart they are instead of making the viewer feel smart; simplify further than feels necessary, and use metaphors and props to compress complex ideas [E].
- Cognitive load theory: working memory handles only a few novel elements at once; load splits into intrinsic (the material's complexity, relative to the viewer's existing knowledge), extraneous (imposed by presentation - the part editing controls), and germane. Because intrinsic load is relative to expertise, the same density that overwhelms a novice is fine for an expert: calibrate density to the audience's knowledge, not to an absolute standard [R: Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019].
- At scale (6.9 million viewing sessions on edX), shorter videos held engagement far better (median engagement dropped steeply after about 6 minutes), informal talking-head footage beat polished studio lectures, and instructors speaking "fairly fast with high enthusiasm" engaged better than slowed-down delivery [R: Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014]. Context: MOOC education, not entertainment - apply to educational content, not universally.

**How a professional applies it.** Editors cut throat-clearing, false starts, and repetition; they order information so each new idea lands on something already established ("hawkeye": broad before deep [E: Kallaway]); they show the thing while it is named; they keep one idea per moment. Film Booth's practical test: say the point and show the point at the same time, even with a few words of text [E].

**When it might NOT apply.** Mystery and curiosity depend on *withheld* clarity: a hook often works by withholding the answer, not the orientation. The viewer should always understand what is happening now; they need not yet understand where it is going. Confusing those two is a common failure: withholding orientation (who/where/what is this) kills attention; withholding resolution (what happens next) creates it.

**How Hermes could apply it.** Track an idea-density budget: one new claim or visual idea per beat. Check each segment: could a first-time viewer, at full playback speed, state what was said and shown? Cut or reorder anything that requires rewatching. Place captions and graphics adjacent to, and simultaneous with, the thing they explain.

**What failure looks like.** Jargon explained three sentences after first use; a graphic that appears after the narration has moved on; three visual ideas competing in one frame; context the title/thumbnail already supplied being re-explained in the first 20 seconds (Film Booth: the packaging does the context work; dive in) [E].

## 3. Storytelling

**What it means.** Raw footage is material; the edit constructs the narrative: what this is about, in what order, with what tension and resolution. In documentary, "the story is created in the editing room" is a working truism [E: Bricca, Documentary Editing].

**Why it matters.** Story is how viewers organize memory and attention. Events understood as part of a story are followed more easily, remembered longer, and felt more strongly than the same events as a list.

**Evidence.**
- Viewers segment continuous activity into events with beginnings and ends; segmentation is hierarchical and viewers largely agree on where boundaries fall. Changes in characters, location, object interaction, goals, and causality predict where boundaries are perceived, in both text and film; boundaries are where processing load spikes (reading slows measurably at boundary clauses) [R: Zacks, Speer & Reynolds 2009]. Editorial implication: a cut placed at a natural event boundary rides the viewer's own segmentation and costs nothing; a cut mid-event is a demand - sometimes exactly the right one (it can go unnoticed under motion, per match-action masking), but it is never free [R + S].
- Narrative must-haves: characters, conflict, progression. Every documentary uses these three in different proportions [E: Bricca].
- Documentary editors organize material by themes and characters and arrive at an act structure from the material, not from a template (Mohamed El Manasterly on The Square) [E].
- Viewers reconstruct the whole from fragments; the edit chooses which fragments, and therefore which whole [R-adjacent, film theory: Rouch via Bricca].

**How a professional applies it.** Read or watch all the material before cutting (documentary editors read the full transcript before touching the timeline [E: ScriptCut/professional doc practice]). Mark the moments that carry weight. Arrange those into a spine (paper edit) that works with no decoration, then build. Open loops early and close them later; delay gratification; anchor the narrative in one clear point of view [E: Bricca's principles].

**When it might NOT apply.** Pure vibe content (mood montage, ASMR, ambient footage) may legitimately have no narrative spine; forcing act structure onto it produces stiffness. Even then, an emotional progression (see Rhythm, Emotion) substitutes for plot.

**How Hermes could apply it.** From the transcript/footage, extract candidate load-bearing moments (the sentence that carries the idea, the reaction that carries the feeling, the action that carries the event). Propose a spine: opening that establishes what this is and why to care, ordered middle that escalates, close that pays off. Verify every kept clip serves the spine; verify the spine works with music and effects muted.

**What failure looks like.** Clips in chronological order because that is how they were shot; a middle that restates the opening at the same energy; an ending that stops rather than resolves; a "story" that is actually a topic (a topic is a subject; a story has tension and change).

## 4. Rhythm

**What it means.** The temporal pattern of the edit: shot lengths, cut density, speech pace, movement, and music working as one felt pulse. Murch: "Editing is 70% about rhythm" (as quoted in Tony Zhou's "How Does an Editor Think and Feel?") [E].

**Why it matters.** Rhythm is how the edit is felt in the body before it is understood. Predictable rhythm disengages; varied rhythm holds. Cutting and DeLong found shot lengths in films cluster in "packets" of similar-length shots with a 1/f (pink noise) pattern across 150 films, and this pattern correlates with attention engagement [R: Cutting, DeLong & Nothelfer 2010]. Uniform metronome cutting is not how engaging films are actually built.

**Evidence.**
- Shot-length clustering (packets, 1/f pattern) in professionally made films [R: Cutting et al. 2010].
- The "attention curve": if every cut lands on the beat, or every shot holds the same length, the audience zones out; contrast (long holds vs rapid sequences, a well-placed pause) resets attention [E: Steed Films].
- Slow cutting builds tension; fast cutting releases or pays it off (Hillier-Smith: tension in the slow cuts, payoff in the fast ones) [E].

**How a professional applies it.** Editors cut to an internal rhythm first (performance, speech cadence, movement) and use music to support it, not the reverse (see audio-editing.md for the beat-cutting debate). They alternate densities: a fast passage earns a slow one. They hold emotional beats longer than informational ones (see Emotion).

**When it might NOT apply.** Some formats intentionally flatten rhythm: lo-fi "slow living" vlogs, ambient background content, or a single-take aesthetic where the absence of cutting is the style.

**How Hermes could apply it.** Compute shot-length distribution of the assembly and compare to intention: is there variation (packets of similar lengths, changes between sections) or is it a flat line? Check cut density against content energy: explanations breathe; highlights accelerate. Do not impose uniform average shot length.

**What failure looks like.** Every clip 2.0 seconds because a template said so; a fast-cut sequence that never lets any shot land; a slow sequence with no internal event to justify the pace; rhythm that fights the music instead of conversing with it.

## 5. Emotion

**What it means.** The edit manages what the viewer feels and when: tension, excitement, humor, curiosity, reflection. Emotion is Murch's top priority: the audience forgives continuity errors, logic gaps, even story confusion if the feeling is right [E].

**Why it matters.** Feeling is what makes content memorable and shared. Hillier-Smith: emotion, story, rhythm in every frame; the difference between comedy and horror is often the music [E].

**Evidence.**
- Emotions take time. Viewers connect to onscreen people because they have time to watch faces before and after speech. Tony Zhou: "Editors have to decide, how much time do I give this emotion?" [E: Every Frame a Painting].
- Music measurably steers interpretation: the same scene with different soundtracks produces different narrative interpretations and anticipated outcomes; viewers interpret a character's emotions through the emotion of the music; music loudness alone can shift interpretation more than changing the piece [R: film-music studies reviewed in Influence of Music on Perceived Emotions in Film].
- Reaction shots are where audience emotion lives: L-cuts exist largely to let us watch a face react while we still hear the words [E: Adobe/professional practice].
- Emotion is more granular than "happy/sad/tense": self-report research using thousands of short evocative videos maps at least 27 distinct emotion categories connected by continuous gradients (awe, amusement, awkwardness, triumph, nostalgia...). An edit that aims for "emotional" in general aims at nothing; name the specific shade [R: Cowen & Keltner 2017].
- Fear has a specific acoustic signature that editors and composers exploit: horror soundtracks over-use harsh, nonlinear sounds (screams, noise-like sidebands) that mimic distress vocalizations, and scream-like music measurably imitates the roughness of actual screams. Drama suppresses noise; horror leans into it [R: Blumstein et al. 2010; Trevor et al. 2020].

**How a professional applies it.** Editors protect emotional time: they hold on the face after the sentence, keep the breath before the answer, let the laugh land before moving on. They build with leitmotifs (recurring sounds/music cues that mark emotional states) [E: Hillier-Smith]. They audition multiple music tracks against the same cut because each activates a different flavor of feeling [E: Steed Films].

**When it might NOT apply.** Pure information delivery (a timestamped tutorial step, a software walkthrough) legitimately prioritizes clarity over feeling; over-emoting a tutorial reads as manipulation.

**How Hermes could apply it.** Detect candidate emotional peaks (prosody shift, laughter, pause, facial change, applause, silence after a statement). At each, ask: does the cut give this moment room? Do not cut on the last syllable of an emotional statement; hold the reaction. Choose music by the feeling of the scene, not by genre label.

**What failure looks like.** Cutting to the next point the instant a sentence ends while the speaker's face is still landing the emotion; music that contradicts the scene's feeling (upbeat track under a vulnerable admission); a joke whose reaction shot is missing.

## 6. Continuity

**What it means.** The illusion that time, space, and action are coherent across cuts: the viewer always knows where they are, who is where, and what is happening.

**Why it matters.** Continuity errors force the viewer out of the story and into puzzling out geography or chronology. The viewer's brain builds no stable map of the scene (viewers are remarkably blind to spatial inconsistency, per change-blindness research), but violations of action and screen direction still disrupt [R: Smith et al. 2012; Frontiers 2023].

**Evidence.**
- Viewers miss many continuity details (change blindness), so editors have latitude; but 180-degree violations measurably disorient: crossing the axis of action triggers an orienting cost in EEG studies [R: Frontiers 2023].
- Match-on-action cuts (cutting during movement) are the least noticeable cuts [R: Smith et al. 2012].
- Jump cuts within a continuous take are read as time passing, and are now standard short-form grammar; they disorient only when action or position is unchanged [E/synthesis].

**How a professional applies it.** Keep screen direction consistent within a scene; cut on action; preserve eyeline logic (a character looking screen-left should be answered by someone looking screen-right); use establishing shots before tight coverage; use cutaways to hide necessary time compression. When compressing speech, hide the visual jump with a cutaway or embrace the jump cut as an intentional style, not a half-hidden accident.

**When it might NOT apply.** Montage, music video, and much short-form deliberately breaks continuity for energy. Jump cuts are the native grammar of YouTube talking-head. The rule is: break continuity deliberately, for effect, consistently - not accidentally.

**How Hermes could apply it.** For multi-angle or multi-take dialogue, track screen direction and do not cross the axis within a scene without a transition that reorients (a wide, a move). Prefer cut points during motion or sound peaks, where the cut is masked. If a time compression is needed mid-take, decide: visible jump cut (style) or covered by B-roll (invisible), never an awkward near-jump.

**What failure looks like.** Two shots of the same speaker from nearly the same angle cut together (a "bad jump" that looks like a glitch); a subject facing left answering someone facing left; an object jumping position between cuts with no cover; chronology of a process shown out of order when comprehension depends on the order.

## 7. Visual hierarchy

**What it means.** Within the frame and across the edit, some elements are dominant: the viewer's eye goes to them first. Framing, scale, contrast, motion, focus, color, and text placement create this order.

**Why it matters.** Hierarchy is attention control inside the shot. If the caption competes with the face, or the graphic competes with the product, the viewer splits attention and gets neither (redundancy and split-attention costs are measurable) [R: Mayer].

**Evidence.**
- Faces and motion dominate attention involuntarily [R: attention research, Smith et al. 2012].
- Printed words near the relevant part of a graphic beat captions at the bottom (spatial contiguity, ES 0.82) [R: Mayer].
- BBC subtitle guidelines cap subtitles at two lines and 160-180 wpm reading speed, position them to avoid covering critical picture information, and break lines at linguistically coherent points - practical hierarchy engineering for text over picture [R/professional standard: BBC].

**How a professional applies it.** One dominant element per frame. Text is sized and timed to be read fully in its display window, then removed. Emphasis graphics (zooms, spotlights, arrows) appear exactly when the narration needs them, then leave. Captions sit in a consistent zone that does not cover the speaker's eyes, the product, or the action.

**When it might NOT apply.** Dense, maximalist styles (some gaming and meme edits) intentionally stack layers; the hierarchy is "everything is loud." This is a style choice, not an accident - but it caps comprehension and suits content where vibe outranks information.

**How Hermes could apply it.** Per frame, identify the intended dominant element (face, product, text, action). Check competitors: is anything brighter, larger, or moving that should not be? Are captions overlapping the dominant element? Resolve conflicts by timing (show text after the visual registers) or by de-emphasis (size, position, duration).

**What failure looks like.** Word-by-word karaoke captions covering the speaker's mouth while a B-roll detail also demands reading; a lower-third title colliding with subtitles; an emphasized callout that stays on screen after the topic moved on.

## 8. Audio

**What it means.** Sound is half the edit: dialogue intelligibility, music, effects, ambience, silence, and how they transition. Full treatment in `audio-editing.md`; the foundation is that audio decisions are editorial decisions, not polish.

**Why it matters.** Viewers tolerate imperfect picture far longer than imperfect sound. Music reshapes what a scene means [R: film-music research]. Bad audio reads as amateur instantly [E, universal professional consensus].

**Evidence.**
- Same scene, different soundtrack: different perceived story [R: film-music studies].
- Audio transitions (J/L cuts, sound bridges) smooth scene changes and manage flow; they are standard professional grammar [E: Adobe, StudioBinder].
- Dialogue first: mixing, ducking, and level decisions serve intelligibility above all [E: professional mixing practice].

**How a professional applies it.** Cut dialogue on speech rhythm, not just picture. Use J/L cuts to keep scenes flowing. Choose music for emotional fit, then fit the cut to the music's phrases where the content allows. Protect silence where it works harder than sound.

**When it might NOT apply.** Silent-foreground content exists (text-driven memes, some ASMR), and sound-off viewing is real on social: captions then carry the load, which changes the visual hierarchy calculus.

**How Hermes could apply it.** Never treat audio as a finishing pass. When evaluating a cut, listen without watching: does the dialogue flow naturally? Does the music change meaning? Are transitions abrupt in a way picture alone would not reveal?

**What failure looks like.** Dialogue cut mid-breath; music that starts and stops with scene changes like an elevator; effects piled on every movement; silence where the content needed a breath and a music sting instead fills it.

## 9. Coherence

**What it means.** Every choice serves the same creative intention. The thumbnail's promise, the hook, the pacing, the music, the caption style, and the ending all belong to the same video.

**Why it matters.** Viewers feel incoherence as untrustworthiness: a serious claim delivered with meme pacing, or a playful vlog cut like a corporate ad, reads as off even when each element is competent.

**Evidence.** Mostly expert consensus and synthesis [E/S]. Kallaway's "common ground" and tone-matching practices, Film Booth's packaging alignment, and Hillier-Smith's "every frame serves emotion/story/rhythm" all point the same direction [E].

**How a professional applies it.** Define the video's promise in one sentence before cutting; test each element against it. The packaging (title/thumbnail) sets a contract; the first seconds confirm it (Kallaway's lock-in zone: confirm the claim, earn trust fast) [E].

**When it might NOT apply.** Deliberate tonal whiplash is a real comedic and dramatic device. It works when the audience can feel the turn as intentional - a setup and a swerve - not as drift.

**How Hermes could apply it.** Keep the brief's intent explicit as a one-line test. For each major element (hook, music, caption style, ending), ask: same video? Flag drift between sections (corporate open, meme middle, sentimental close).

**What failure looks like.** A clickbait hook the video never pays off; a somber topic with hype music; an ending that promises a different video than the one delivered.

## 10. Restraint

**What it means.** Knowing when not to cut, not to zoom, not to add the effect, not to lay the music sting. Editorial restraint is a skill, and its absence is the single most common marker of automated editing (see `common-ai-editing-failures.md`).

**Why it matters.** Every element added spends viewer attention. Effects that do not serve understanding or feeling actively harm both (Mayer's coherence principle is the lab version of this) [R]. Hillier-Smith on Civil War: the power move was letting the audience settle into the film's language and then taking it away - less is more, timed deliberately [E].

**Evidence.**
- Coherence principle: extraneous additions measurably reduce learning [R: Mayer].
- "If your video only works with a soundtrack, the story isn't strong enough" [E: Steed Films].
- Editors warn against overcutting: oversimplification in the polish stage can "lose the heart" of a film [E: Mary Lampson via Bricca, Documentary Editing].

**How a professional applies it.** Defaults: hard cut over transition, no zoom over zoom, no sting over sting, silence over filler music, the speaker's face over decorative B-roll when the face carries the meaning. Add an element only when a specific moment asks for it.

**When it might NOT apply.** High-energy genres (sports reels, gaming montages, hype ads) have a higher baseline of acceptable density. Restraint there means discipline within the energy: not every kill gets a slow-mo, not every beat gets a flash.

**How Hermes could apply it.** For every proposed element beyond cutting and audio cleanup, require a one-line justification tied to viewer understanding or feeling. If the justification is "adds energy" or "makes it dynamic" without a target moment, reject it. Count elements per minute and investigate outliers against the content type's norms.

**What failure looks like.** A talking-head clip with zooms, whooshes, and caption animations every few seconds that viewers describe as "feeling AI-generated"; a montage so dense no single image registers.

## Cross-principle conflicts and how to resolve them

- **Clarity vs. Story/Emotion:** withhold resolution (story), never orientation (clarity).
- **Rhythm vs. Emotion:** emotion wins. If the feeling needs one more second, the rhythm pattern breaks there (Murch's hierarchy) [E].
- **Continuity vs. Rhythm:** a slightly imperfect continuity match on the right beat is usually better than a perfect match on the wrong beat - because viewers miss continuity errors but feel rhythm [R: change blindness; E: Murch].
- **Attention vs. Restraint:** the strongest attention tool is a well-chosen moment, not an effect. Use effects to point, not to shout.
